Part 1 · Reality

It is not a fact engine.
It is a language probability engine.

One task only

Predicting the word most likely to come next.

No ground truth

It does not “know” a source exists. It produces the convincing shape of one.

Confidence is a style

The assured tone comes from academic prose patterns — not from certainty.

So what is actually happening when it answers? Let's watch.

Part 1 · Reality  ·  What compounds

When the output becomes the input

One image, asked to be recreated exactly, 101 times.
Each output became the next input.
Clip: demonstration by Linas Beliunas, published on LinkedIn (2026). Reproduced for teaching with attribution.

No new information ever enters

Each pass keeps what is probable and drops what is rare. Fine detail dies first; only bold, high-frequency shapes survive.

The accurate name for this

Model collapse, not drift. Drift is the world moving around a fixed model; collapse is a model trained on its own output, losing the tails.

Why this reaches your discipline

About 146,900 fabricated citations entered the record in 2025 — and that record is training data. Local scholarship sits in the tails, and tails go first.

Careful, original, human-collected data is what stops this. Not a sentimental claim — it is the finding.

Clip compressed and silenced for this deck; the printed PDF substitutes four sampled frames. Model collapse: Shumailov, I., et al. (2024). AI models collapse when trained on recursively generated data. Nature, 631, 755–759.

Direct consequence · 3 of 3

Once it leaves, you no longer know where it goes

×

UKM Generative AI Guideline (2025)

Never enter these into public AI tools

Human data

Interview transcripts, recordings, case notes, contact details — anything that could identify a participant.

Student material

Student records and assessment material not already public.

Others' confidential work

Unpublished manuscripts, embargoed findings, grant proposals, peer reviews, examiner reports.

Third-party property

Instruments, datasets, code or material under a confidentiality obligation.

Default position when in doubt: do not upload.

Seven stages, two columns

The right column is not merely a moral prohibition — every item is a task the model cannot perform reliably.

Part 2 · Demonstration

Two ways of asking. Two kinds of answer.

The first way

Find five articles on household financial literacy in Malaysia.
Author, A. (2021). A plausible title…
Author, B. (2019). A plausible title…
Author, C. (2022). A plausible title…
Two of them do not exist. You cannot tell which two.

The second way

Using only the five articles I have attached: list the construct each one uses, the instrument measured, and any contradictions between them. If something is not in the text, write “not stated”. Do not add other references.
Article 1 — construct: financial knowledge; instrument: 13-item scale
Article 2 — construct: financial behaviour; instrument: not stated
Contradiction: two different definitions of the same construct
Every line traces back to text you supplied.

The difference is not prompt length. It is where the information came from — from you, or from the model's memory.

Part 2 · Skill

Five parts — and the template they build

1

Context

Who you are, what study, for which reader.

2

A fixed source

The material you supply — and a ban on anything else.

3

One task verb

List, compare, classify. Not “please help me”.

4

Output format

Fields you specify — so the gaps become visible.

5

An uncertainty rule

The single sentence that saves the most trouble.

Template — copy it, replace the coloured parts
1

I am a [role] at [faculty], writing [type of output] on [research topic].

2

Use ONLY the material I have attached below. Do not add sources, references or figures from anywhere else.

3

List the construct and instrument used by each article, and state any contradictions between them.

4

Return a table with the fields: Article | Construct | Instrument | Sample size | Main finding.

5

If a field is not in the text I supplied, write “not stated”. Do not estimate and do not fill gaps.

Four of these five parts are your work — knowledge of your own study. Which is why a good prompt cannot simply be copied from someone else.

Part 2 · Skill

Six patterns worth keeping

PATTERN 1

Extract to a table

“From the attached, extract [fields] into a table. Write not stated where absent.”

Why: gaps become visible.

PATTERN 2

Steelman the argument

“Give the three strongest objections a critical reviewer would raise.”

Why: nothing leaves; rehearses Reviewer 2.

PATTERN 3

Find the disagreement

“Where do these sources conflict? Quote the conflicting lines.”

Why: undoes the flattening of debate.

PATTERN 4

Explain, then verify

“Explain [concept], then list what I should verify independently.”

Why: output becomes a checklist.

PATTERN 5

Translate & back-translate

“Translate these items, then translate your translation back.”

Why: surfaces drift early.

PATTERN 6

Structure, don't write

“Propose three alternative outlines. Outlines only — no prose.”

Why: the sentences stay yours.

What every pattern has in common: you supply the material, and the model does something structural to it.

Part 2 · Choosing tools

Choose by engine type, not by brand

TYPE 1 · GENERATIVE

No search

Good for drafting, structure, explanation and code. Dangerous for anything needing a source.

e.g. ChatGPT · Claude · Gemini · Copilot

TYPE 2 · RETRIEVAL-GROUNDED

Fetches first, answers second

Good for finding literature and querying your own documents. A citation may exist yet not support the claim.

e.g. Elicit · Consensus · Scite · Semantic Scholar · NotebookLM

TYPE 3 · SPECIALISED

Built for one task

Screening, extraction, references, qualitative coding. Narrow scope, far easier to audit and report.

e.g. Rayyan · Covidence · ASReview · Zotero · NVivo / MAXQDA / ATLAS.ti

TYPE 4 · HYBRID & AGENTIC

Searches, runs code, chains steps

Most capable, hardest to audit. Condition: log every step, or you cannot write your Methods section.

e.g. deep-research modes in major assistants; agents that browse and execute code

TYPE 5 · LOCAL

Runs on your own machine

Data never leaves. Lower performance, but often the only legitimate option for participant data.

e.g. Ollama · LM Studio · local-first QDA tools

The first question is not “which is smartest”, but: does it search? can it be audited? does my data leave?

Examples current as of August 2026 — illustrations of each type, not endorsements. If a tool shows no links to real documents, it is a generative engine.

Part 2 · Worked example A

Literature screening that survives review

1

Search in a real database first

Scopus, Web of Science, EconLit. AI does not replace the search — it reorders what the search returned.

2

Deduplicate and import to a screening tool

Rayyan, Covidence or ASReview — decisions stay in one auditable, exportable place.

3

Let AI rank relevance; you screen

Ranking changes the order you read in. It does not change what is included.

4

Hand-screen a random 10% and compute agreement

This single step converts “I used AI” into a reportable method.

5

Report tool, version, settings and agreement rate

In Methods, alongside your PRISMA flow.

AI can reorder the pile. It cannot decide what is in it. Keep the inclusion decision human and the disagreement rate visible.

Part 2 · Worked example B

Qualitative coding: a second lens, not a second coder

1

You code a subset by hand first

Your codebook exists before any tool sees the data — otherwise the model's categories quietly become yours.

2

Anonymise — or keep it local

Strip identifiers, or run a local model so transcripts never leave the machine.

3

Ask for candidate themes on that subset

Require a supporting quote for every theme, so each one is traceable.

4

Compare with your codebook — and keep the conflicts

Where you disagree, write an analytic memo. That memo is the finding.

5

Report which step AI touched and how you validated it

UKM's baseline standard: validation must be reported, not merely performed.

The value is in the disagreement, not the agreement. If the model only confirms what you already coded, it has told you nothing.

Part 2 · Worked example C

Analysis code: the fastest win, with one rule

1

Describe the data, not the data itself

Variable names, types and structure are enough. Raw rows are rarely needed.

2

Ask for commented code, run it on a subset

Stata, R or Python. The comments are how you learn what it assumed.

3

Check one result by hand

One mean, one cross-tab, one coefficient. If it matches, continue.

4

Ask for the same analysis a second way, then compare

Divergence between two routes is the cheapest bug detector you have.

Why this is the safest use of all

No participant data need be uploaded, the output is testable against a known answer, and errors announce themselves as errors rather than as fluent prose.

The one rule

Code that runs is not code that is correct. A silent logic error still produces a table — and the table still looks publishable.

You are responsible for every line, including the ones you did not type.

Where AI presses on research ethics

Confidentiality

LINE 4

Validity

Can it be repeated?

Justice

Each one already has an official document behind it. You do not need to invent policy.

Boundary line 1

Accountability cannot be transferred to a tool

A consistent position

  • COPE: AI tools cannot be listed as authors.
  • MOHE (2024): generative AI technology may not be listed as a co-author of any academic output.
  • UKM (2025): using a tool does not transfer responsibility to the tool's provider.

A simple test

If you cannot defend a sentence in a viva or an integrity inquiry, that sentence does not yet belong in your manuscript — no matter who typed it.

Boundary line 2

Not every use needs to be declared

ASSISTIVE · no declaration
  • Grammar, spelling, thesaurus
  • Smoothing sentences you wrote yourself
  • Reference formatting
GENERATIVE · must be declared
  • Substantive draft text
  • Literature summaries entering the manuscript
  • Analysis code and data coding
  • Suggested themes, interpretations, arguments
  • Translation of research instruments

The line is not how much AI was used. The line is whether AI contributed intellectual content.

Declared in the Methods and Acknowledgements sections. For UKM theses: Appendix B2 of the UKM Generative AI Guideline.

Boundary line 3

Participant data is not yours to give away

Participants consented to you, for the purpose you stated, under conditions your ethics committee approved.

Coding is not anonymisation

In a small population, context alone can identify a participant.

Transcription counts

A 2025 research-ethics editorial recommends informing participants and obtaining explicit consent.

Peer review

NIH prohibits reviewers from uploading application content to online AI tools.

“If my participants reread the form they signed, would they expect their words to be sent to a foreign company's servers?”

Boundary line 4

If it cannot be repeated, it is not a method

Qualitative coding

It can be a second lens. It cannot be a second coder.

Review screening

It can speed things up — if accuracy rates are reported and human cross-checked.

Analysis code

Subtle bugs still produce output. You test every line.

What must be recorded

Tool · version · date · prompt · data supplied · output accepted · how it was verified.

Boundary line 5 · Justice and local voice

Instrument 1 · Zones of use

Traffic lights, mapped to real FEP work

Red

  • Identifiable transcripts
  • Manuscripts or theses under review
  • Uncollected data reported as real
  • Citing a source you have not opened

Amber

  • Literature summaries — every source checked
  • Theme suggestions on anonymised data
  • Review screening — cross-validated
  • Instrument translation — with back-translation
  • Condition: declared and recorded.

Green

  • Grammar and spelling
  • Reorganising your own notes
  • Explaining concepts you then verify
  • Playing devil's advocate against your argument

An honest note: the UKM guideline itself warns that traffic-light models create an “enforcement illusion” — this is a communication tool, not a control mechanism.

Instrument 4b

The actual words you can copy

Methods section
[Tool, version] was used on [date] for [task]. All output was verified through [verification method]. The authors take full responsibility for the accuracy and final interpretation.
Participant consent form
Interview transcripts will be processed using [tool]. Your data will be [anonymised before processing]. You may decline automated processing without affecting your participation.

Three rules for good disclosure

Specific — name the actual task  ·  Assessable — readers can judge its effect  ·  State the verification — what you did to check it.

05
Four cases
You vote with a show of hands — red, amber or green. All four happened at real universities.
1
Case to judge

Interview transcripts

A researcher interviewed 28 micro-entrepreneurs in one district in Selangor. She uploaded all the transcripts to a public AI tool for theme suggestions. Participant names were replaced with codes (P1, P2, P3…). Ethics approval was granted a year ago.
RED

Coding is not anonymisation. Among 28 entrepreneurs in one district, business type and contextual detail identify individuals easily.

Approval a year ago does not cover this. The consent form almost certainly says nothing about overseas third-party processing.

What should have happened: thorough anonymisation and a local model; or a protocol amendment to the ethics committee.

2
Case to judge

Instrument translation

A lecturer used AI to translate a 22-item financial wellbeing scale into Malay. The translation was read once, looked fluent, and went straight out to 400 respondents. No participant data was entered — only published scale items.
AMBER → RED

The translating: amber. No participant data was exposed, and assisted translation is a legitimate starting point if declared.

Distributing it unvalidated: red. This is a validity failure. Semantic equivalence is not psychometric equivalence — and 400 responses cannot be rescued afterwards.

What should have happened: back-translation, expert panel, cognitive pilot, and reported reliability for the Malay version.

3
Case to judge

Peer review

A professor received a manuscript to review. The deadline was close. He uploaded the PDF to an AI tool for a summary and a list of methodological weaknesses, then wrote his review himself based on that list. The final review is entirely his own writing.
RED

The breach happened at the moment of upload, not in the writing. An unpublished manuscript is confidential work entrusted to a reviewer.

Explicitly prohibited. NIH bars reviewers from uploading application content; UKM lists unpublished manuscripts among prohibited material.

Writing it himself restores nothing. If time does not permit a proper review, the right answer is to decline the invitation.

4
Case to judge

Thesis language editing

A PhD candidate used an AI tool to improve grammar and sentence flow in her discussion chapter. The argument, structure and interpretation are entirely hers. She did not mention it, considering it equivalent to a spell checker.
GREEN — with two conditions

The use is legitimate. Language improvement on human-authored text is assistive use; MOHE places no restriction on proofreading tools.

First condition: supervision. UKM requires supervisor permission for material use in a thesis, and a supervisor may restrict it early on to protect the development of writing skill.

Second condition: it stays green only while it is genuinely language. Once the tool starts suggesting arguments, it has crossed into amber.

Part 6 · Reframing
What can be automated will become cheap. What demands judgement will become expensive.

What remains valuable is what cannot be generated: choosing the right question, recognising suspicious data, understanding context that exists in no corpus, and being willing to say “I am not yet sure”.

Appendix A · For distribution

Checklist before using AI

Before you start

  • UKM, funder and target journal policies checked
  • Permitted tools agreed with your supervisor
  • Institutional account, or a local model for sensitive data
  • Retention and training settings checked

While using it

  • Only the minimum information supplied
  • Data anonymised before any tool touches it
  • Tool, version, date and prompt recorded
  • Every source opened and read before citing

Before you submit

  • Every reference verified against the original source
  • Analysis output verified and the step reported
  • Disclosure included in Methods and Acknowledgements
  • Appendix B2 completed if this is thesis work

The three-question test

  • Is this data mine to give away?
  • Can I defend this output without the AI?
  • Am I willing to write it in the Methods section?

Three yeses — proceed and declare. One no — stop.

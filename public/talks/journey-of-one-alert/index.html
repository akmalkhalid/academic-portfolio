The rain did not stop for three days.

Block A · Computer Science · CLO 1

The decision

When should the siren sound?

Block A

Both mistakes cost lives — differently

  • Warn too late — people are trapped by rising water.
  • Warn too often — after three false alarms, nobody moves for the fourth. The real one.

A computer makes this call at 3 a.m., in the rain, with nobody watching. What exactly should it do? Someone must write it down — completely.

Block A · Your turn

Write the alert rule

Every 15 min you get two numbers: river level (m) and rainfall upstream (mm/h). Danger is 28.0 m.

In pairs — write the exact, numbered rule. It must satisfy all four:

  1. fire once per flood (never spam), yet still fire afresh if a second flood arrives later;
  2. use the rainfall too — warn earlier when the level is rising fast under heavy rain;
  3. survive a sensor returning −3 m, or a flat unchanging value (is the river calm, or the gauge dead?);
  4. never hinge on a difference the gauge can't actually see (you'll test this next block).

⏱ 8 minutes

  • What single condition — an invariant — makes requirements ① and ② true at once? Tie "already warned" to a level that must first fall back below a lower line before you re-arm.
  • Why must the warn line (28.0) and the re-arm line (say 27.5) differ? What flicker does that gap kill when the river hovers right at 28.0?
  • The reading never arrives — pure silence. Should your rule treat "no data" as safe, or as its own alarm?

Block A

You just wrote an algorithm — under pressure

An algorithm is a precise, ordered, complete procedure an executor with no judgment can follow. The hard part was never the arithmetic — it was making one rule meet all four demands at once.

# sanity FIRST · use rainfall · fire once · re-arm below a lower line if level < 0 or level > 35: log("sensor fault"); skip cycle # ③ rise = level − level_prev if not alerted: if level >= 28.0: send("EVACUATE"); alerted = True # ① elif level >= 27.0 and (rise >= 0.15 or rain_mmh >= 20): send("ADVISORY") # ② rain warns earlier if alerted and level < 27.5: alerted = False # ① re-arm for the next flood

Warn at 28.0, stand down only below 27.5 — that gap (hysteresis) stops the siren flickering when the river sits right at the line. ④ waits for the next block: never hinge on a difference the gauge can't see.

◉ Real-world stakesBoeing's 737 MAX let one system act on a single sensor reading with no sanity cross-check. Two crashes, 346 lives. The "handle the crazy reading" line you wrote is the line they were missing.

Block A deep dive

How do you know a rule is right?

  • Precondition — what must hold before a step (the level passed the sanity check).
  • Postcondition — what the step guarantees after (EVACUATE sent iff level ≥ 28.0 and not already alerted).
  • Invariant — true after every cycle: "alerted is true exactly when an SMS was sent and the level hasn't since dropped below 27.5."
◉ Real-world stakesThe Therac-25 radiation machine was "tested and working" — but an unreasoned timing flaw delivered fatal overdoses. Running it once is not knowing it's correct.
⇄ Connects to Block CThis invariant is not abstract — in an hour it becomes the literal line and not alert_sent. The proof and the code are the same thought.

Block A deep dive

How much work is your rule?

Big-O names how work grows with input. A few checks per reading is O(1) — instant forever. Re-scanning the whole flood history each reading is O(n²) — fine in a demo, ruinous in a monsoon.

⇄ Connects to Block D · securityThe extreme is exponential, O(2ⁿ): cracking an n-character password. Each extra character doubles the attacker's work — which is why length beats cleverness, and why the padlock's 2¹²⁸ key is safe. Complexity is a security tool.

Block B · Computer Science + Digital Technology · CLO 2

The measurement & the machine

How a river becomes numbers

Block B · Your turn

What does the river say to the machine?

The gauge sends the level as a 12-bit number (0–4095) mapped onto 0–35 m.

  • In pairs: (a) how big is one step, in mm? (b) the town must catch a 1 cm rise — can 12 bits see it? (c) the gauge also jitters by ±2 steps of noise — now what is the smallest change you can actually trust?

⏱ 6 minutes

(a) 35 m ÷ 4096 ≈ 8.5 mm. (b) 1 cm = 10 mm is bigger than one step, so a clean sensor can just see it. (c) but ±2 steps ≈ ±17 mm of noise swamps a 10 mm change — you can only trust a jump beyond ~4 steps ≈ 3.4 cm, unless you average many readings. Resolution is the ceiling; noise pulls the real limit lower.

To resolve 1 mm across 0–35 m you'd need 35,000 levels → 16 bits (2¹⁶ = 65,536). Storage: 12 bits × 96 readings/day = 144 bytes/day — which is why the sensor log is nearly free while the CCTV is not.

Next slide is live — check part (a) by dragging the river yourself.

Block B

The map is not the river

2^12 = 4096 values over 0–35 m step ≈ 8.5 mm error ≈ ±4.3 mm +noise ±2 steps → trust ≥ ~3.4 cm

28.000 and 28.004 m differ by 4 mm — smaller than one step, so they share one code. And 8.5 mm is only the ceiling: with ±2 steps of noise, the smallest change you can actually trust is ~4 steps ≈ 3.4 cm. Resolution caps precision; noise lowers it further.

Wherebitswhat one step means
This flood gauge12≈ 8.5 mm of river

Phone camera (per colour)8256 shades — too few and a clear sky shows "banding"

More bits = finer truth, but more data to store and move. Every sensor design picks a point on this line.

◉ Real-world stakesThe same limit sets what an ECG sees in a heartbeat, what a bridge's strain sensor detects before a crack, and how finely a self-driving car ranges a pedestrian. Too few bits, and the danger is invisible.

Block B

Two symbols carry everything

Inside the machine every switch is on or off. One choice = a bit; eight = a byte; four bits = one hex digit (so 3276 = 0xCCC, and every #F0A929 colour).

110011001100

CCTV frame, SMS text, siren command — all become numbers, all numbers become bits. One alphabet: binary.

⇄ Connects to Block DBecause everything is the same bits, a message can be chopped into numbered packets and rebuilt anywhere. Data representation is the quiet precondition that makes the entire internet possible.

Block B deep dive

Representing the impossible — and the imprecise

The −3 m fault

Two's complement: invert the bits of +3, add 1.

+3 = 0000 0011 → 1111 1100 (invert) → 1111 1101 (+1) = −3

Addition works across zero with no extra circuit.

The 28.1 m reading

28.1 has no exact finite binary form — like ⅓ in decimal. As floating point it rounds:

0.1 + 0.2 = 0.30000000000000004

Never test a level for exact equality. Use ≥ and a tolerance.

Block B · why this is not a footnote

When a rounding error costs everything

Patriot missile · 1991

Time in a fixed-point counter; 0.1 s isn't exact in binary. After ~100 h the clock drifted ~0.34 s — a Scud flies ~500 m in that gap. The interceptor missed. 28 soldiers died.

Ariane 5 · 1996

A 64-bit float velocity crammed into a 16-bit integer overflowed; guidance shut down; the rocket tore itself apart 37 s after launch. ~US$370 million.

◉ Vancouver Stock Exchange · 1983Its index truncated instead of rounding on every recalculation. Over ~22 months the error compounded: the index read ~520 while the market had actually risen. Corrected overnight to ~1098 — the "missing" half was pure rounding. Same bug in your bank balance, at scale.

The ±4 mm blindness in your gauge — scaled up — is a war casualty, a lost rocket, and a broken stock index. Precision is a discipline, not pedantry.

Block B deep dive

One alphabet: text and images too

Text → ASCII / Unicode

'S' = 83 = 0101 0011 'O' = 79 = 0100 1111 'S' = 83 = 0101 0011

Unicode extends it to 中文, عربي, emoji — 140,000+ characters, one scheme.

Image → pixels

Each pixel = 3 numbers (R,G,B) × 8 bits = 24 bits. A 1920×1080 still:

1920×1080×24 ÷ 8 ≈ 6.22 MB raw ≈ 597 MB / day (96 stills)

Sensor log = 144 bytes/day, image = megabytes — three media, two symbols. That's "digital".

Block B deep dive

The machine that reads them

Input→Process→Output→Storage; the CPU runs fetch→decode→execute billions of times a second. Von Neumann's idea — code and data share one memory — is why software is so powerful… and why a virus (data treated as code) is even possible.

One turn of the cycle for "is the level past danger?" — repeated billions of times a second:

FETCH grab the next instruction from memory (compare level, 28.0) DECODE work out what it means ("subtract, then check the sign") EXECUTE do it in the ALU (level − 28.0 → set a flag) store result · advance · repeat

Memory hierarchy (fast+tiny → slow+huge): registers → cache → RAM → disk. Each step is ~10–100× slower but far larger, so the CPU keeps hot data close. That gap is why "the program feels slow" is usually a memory story, not a CPU one.

Block C · Basic Programming · CLO 3

The script

Programming the alert controller

Block C

Your rule had human readers. The controller does not.

A program is an algorithm written in a language a machine performs exactly. It cannot guess. It forgives nothing.

At 3 a.m., in the rain, that is precisely what you want.

Block C · Your turn

The controller — it runs, and it is wrong

danger = 28.0 alert_sent = False while running: level = read_gauge() if level > danger: send_sms("EVACUATE") if level < -1 or level > 35: log("sensor fault")

In pairs:

  • ① Find three flaws — one floods, one trusts a broken sensor, and one lets a real danger slip through silently.
  • ② Hand-trace it on [28.0, 28.3, 28.2, 27.4, 28.1] — how many SMS does this buggy version send, and does it warn at the right moments?

⏱ 9 minutes

  • Three flaws: no guard (re-sends every cycle); the sanity check runs after the alert (a 40 m glitch fires first); and > should be >= — a reading of exactly 28.0 is ignored.
  • Buggy trace → 3 SMS (28.3, 28.2, 28.1) — and it misses the real onset at 28.0, then floods. A correct controller sends 2: at 28.0, then again at 28.1 after the 27.4 reset. Wrong count and wrong moments.

Block C

Three bugs — and the fix

while running: level = read_gauge() if level < -1 or level > 35: log("fault"); continue # sanity FIRST if level >= danger and not alert_sent: send_sms("EVACUATE"); alert_sent = True # guard + >= catches exactly 28.0 if level < 27.5: alert_sent = False # reset re-arms

Three fixes in three lines: sanity first; the guard so it fires once; and >= not >, so a reading of exactly 28.0 still counts.

◉ Real-world stakesHospitals log hundreds of alarms per patient per day; overwhelmed staff tune them out. "Alarm fatigue" is a recognised cause of patient deaths — Bug 1, in a ward.

Block C deep dive

Every program is three structures

Böhm–Jacopini (1966): any computable procedure needs only these three, nested.

Sequence = the guarded steps · Selection = each if · Iteration = the while. Your controller, WhatsApp, and a Mars rover — same three.

Block C deep dive

Variables, types, and logic

A variable is a named box that remembers; its type says what fits: float level, bool alert_sent, str "EVACUATE". The "faulty" test negates by De Morgan's law:

not (level < -1 or level > 35) ≡ (level >= -1) and (level <= 35)

Prove De Morgan by truth table — the two columns match in all four rows:

AB¬(A ∨ B)(¬A) ∧ (¬B)
FFTT
FTFF
TFFF
TTFF

The mirror law: ¬(A ∧ B) ≡ (¬A) ∨ (¬B). Now lift it from one reading to a whole stream, with quantifiers (predicate logic):

∀ r ∈ window: −1 ≤ r ≤ 35 # FOR ALL readings, sane ∃ r ∈ window: r ≥ 28.0 # THERE EXISTS a danger reading ¬(∀ r: sane(r)) ≡ ∃ r: ¬sane(r) # De Morgan, quantified

"All readings sane" fails exactly when "some reading is faulty" — the same law, over a stream. This is the logic that formal-verification tools run on real safety-critical systems.

◉ Real-world stakesNASA's Mars Climate Orbiter (1999) burned up because one team used imperial units and another metric — a type/unit mismatch nobody caught. US$327 million, gone.
⇄ Connects to Block D · securityWhen a string is mistakenly run as code, that's SQL injection — the internet's most common attack, and just a type confusion an attacker triggers on purpose. Types are a safety boundary.

Block C deep dive

The controller is a finite state machine

Two states, two transitions. The 28.0 / 27.5 gap is the hysteresis that stops the flicker.

Real controllers hold more states. Add FAULT (a bad sensor) and the whole behaviour is just a small table:

In stateevent→ go to
NORMALlevel ≥ 28.0ALERTED (send SMS)
NORMAL / ALERTEDreading out of rangeFAULT (log, page a human)
ALERTEDlevel < 27.5NORMAL (all clear)
FAULTreadings sane againNORMAL

A finite state machine is this table. Draw it and you've specified the program before writing a single line.

⇄ Connects to Block DTCP is a finite state machine too — same circles-and-arrows diagram, new domain. Learn the shape once and you see it in traffic lights, vending machines, and the protocol carrying your alert.

Block C deep dive

Testing & debugging — a discipline, not luck

  • Testing: run against inputs whose answer you already know (a test oracle) — the storm must give exactly 2 SMS. Good tests hit edge cases: exactly 28.0, a fault mid-flood, a value on the reset line.
  • Debugging: reproduce → isolate the smallest failing case → hypothesise → fix → re-run every test (a fix that breaks an old case is a regression).
◉ Real-world stakesKnight Capital (2012) deployed a change without catching one stale code path. Bad trades poured out for 45 minutes — US$440 million, and the firm nearly collapsed by lunch.

Block D · Digital Technology · CLO 4

Delivery & deception

The internet, and who abuses it

Block D

Your alert travels as pieces, not as a message

Each device has an IP address (the delivery label). Your phone (client) asks; the alert system's big computer (server) answers. "The cloud" is that server — someone else's computer, in a building.

Ask any computer to trace the path to a server and it lists every device the packets pass through:

# traceroute to the alert server 1 192.168.0.1 1 ms # your home router (private) 2 10.x.x.x 8 ms # your ISP 3 ... 15 ms # national backbone 7 103.x.x.x 42 ms # the data centre 8 server 43 ms # "the cloud" — a real machine

No magic — just a chain of other people's computers, each a hop closer. And every hop is a place a packet could be delayed, dropped, or watched.

◉ Real-world stakesPackets picking their own routes is why the internet survives cut cables and dead routers — knock out one path, the pieces flow around it. Resilience was the design goal.

Block D deep dive

The layered internet

Application
the alert itself — SMS, web, email. What humans care about.
Transport
TCP (reliable, ordered) or UDP (fast, lossy). Splits data into segments.
Internet
IP — addressing and routing. Labels each packet and finds a path.
Link
the actual wire, fibre, or radio to the next device (wifi, 4G).

Each layer trusts the one below and serves the one above — so new wifi hardware never disturbs the SMS app. Envelopes inside envelopes.

Block D deep dive

Your two addresses, explained

Private · 192.168.x.x

The label inside your network (wifi settings). Reused in every home; unreachable from outside.

Public · what-is-my-IP

The one address your router shows the world, shared by everyone behind it. NAT translates between them.

IPv4 = 32 bits ≈ 4.3 billion, now exhausted → NAT and 128-bit IPv6. A port says which program; DNS is the phone book turning bank.com.my into a number.

Try it (2 min): wifi settings vs "what is my IP" — you'll see both addresses.

Block D deep dive

Reliability from unreliable parts

TCP rebuilds guaranteed delivery from sequence numbers, acknowledgements, and timeout + retransmit.

TCP — the SMS: must arrive, in order, even late. UDP — live flood video: a late frame is useless, so drop it.

⇄ Connects to Block CThat "lost → resend" logic is a finite state machine — the same circles-and-arrows you drew for the controller. You already know how to read this.

Block D · the hinge

The network delivers by address.
It verifies nothing about honesty.

That single design fact is why Malaysians lose billions of ringgit a year to scams. Anyone can send a message that claims to be your bank, LHDN, or a flood authority.

Which part of CIA is under attack? Block D deep dive

Security principles

  • Confidentiality — only the intended reader.
  • Integrity — unaltered in transit.
  • Availability — there when needed.

2FA = something you know + have + are. That's why the scammer must ask for your TAC — the one factor they can't steal remotely.

◉ Real-world stakesA flood-alert server knocked offline by a denial-of-service attack fails Availability — true, correct, and never arrives. All three legs must hold at once.

Block D deep dive

Caesar → Kerckhoffs → the padlock

Kerckhoffs's principle: assume the enemy knows the algorithm; only the key stays secret. Modern ciphers publish the method and keep the key at 2¹²⁸. The browser padlock (TLS) certifies an encrypted line to the named domain — not that the domain is honest.

◉ Real-world stakesMost phishing sites now carry a valid padlock. bank-verify-my.com can be fully encrypted and a pure scam. The padlock means "no one is eavesdropping" — never "trust this site."

Block D

Three habits, one phone number

  • Phishing tells: manufactured urgency, authority-claiming names, mismatched links — and the unfakeable one, the ask for a password or TAC.
  • Passphrases + 2FA: long beats clever; never reused; second factor on email first. Never read a TAC aloud — a spoken TAC is money leaving.
  • Digital footprint: what you post stays reachable and searchable — future employers included.
  • If money moved: call the National Scam Response Centre — 997 — immediately. Minutes matter.

  • a link to "confirm" — the real bank never sends you to one.
  • "Kongsi TAC" — the unfakeable tell: no honest party asks for your code.

Every surface detail can be perfected. The ask is the one thing a forgery can't hide.

Block D · formative check

Apply it (last check)

  1. A call from your bank's real number (caller ID) asks for your TAC. Give two reasons from today it is still a scam.
  2. A site has a valid padlock and reads bank-verify-my.com. Do you enter your password? Why?
  3. A friend reused one password everywhere and it leaked. Why does 2FA on their email still save them?
  1. Caller ID is an address — spoofable, since the network checks address not identity; and asking for a TAC is the unfakeable tell no honest party needs.
  2. No. The padlock only means the line is encrypted to that domain — and that domain is a look-alike, not the bank.
  3. The leaked password alone can't pass the second factor; email is the recovery hub for everything else, so protecting it first contains the damage.

Final check

Three questions, two hours

  • 1. Algorithm vs. program — and why the edge cases matter more than the happy path. (A, C)
  • 2. The four stages — and why a 12-bit sensor puts a floor under your decision rule. (B)
  • 3. The internet checks addresses, not honesty. Connect that to a message asking your friend for their TAC. (D)

⏱ 5 minutes · memory first

All three are the same lesson wearing different clothes: a computer does exactly what its rules say, and trusts exactly what it's told to trust — so the whole craft is choosing those rules and that trust with care.
